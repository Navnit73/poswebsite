Australian community pharmacy is defined by two forces: the **Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS)**, which sets prices and reimbursement for most prescription medicines, and intense retail competition from discount chains. Independent owners are squeezed on both sides, which makes disciplined **pharmacy inventory management** one of the most practical ways to protect profit.

This guide looks at the Australian factors that shape stock decisions and how a small pharmacy can run a leaner, safer stock room.

---

## Australia's Medicine Schedules and What They Mean for Stock

Medicines in Australia are classified under the Poisons Standard (SUSMP). Each schedule affects how stock is stored, sold, and recorded:

| Schedule | Name | Stock Implications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **S2** | Pharmacy Medicine | Sold in pharmacy; often self-selection; standard expiry control |
| **S3** | Pharmacist Only Medicine | Behind the counter; pharmacist involvement at sale |
| **S4** | Prescription Only Medicine | Dispensed on prescription |
| **S8** | Controlled Drug | Locked storage, drug register, strict reconciliation |

Your inventory system should let you flag schedules and prescription requirements on every product, so counter staff can see at a glance what they can and can't sell without a pharmacist. ## S8 Drug Registers and Reconciliation

S8 record-keeping is regulated at the **state and territory** level, so details differ between NSW, Victoria, Queensland, WA, and the rest. Common themes include:

- A **drug register** recording every receipt and supply, with a running balance
- **Regular balance checks** — frequency depends on your jurisdiction
- **Destruction of expired S8 stock** only with an authorised witness
- Integration with **real-time prescription monitoring** (such as SafeScript in Victoria)

Your legal S8 register must meet your state's requirements. A stock system with timestamped adjustments and per-user accountability makes discrepancies far easier to investigate.

## How 60-Day Dispensing Changed Stock Planning

From September 2023, the Government phased in **60-day dispensing** for a list of PBS medicines for stable chronic conditions. Eligible patients can collect two months' supply per dispensing instead of one.

For stock planning, that means:

- **Lumpier demand** — fewer, larger dispensing events for affected lines
- **Higher stock-holding requirements** per patient at the moment of dispensing
- **Re-calibrated reorder points**, because historic monthly averages no longer reflect dispensing patterns

If your reorder points were set before 2023, they're probably wrong for these lines. Our **[reorder point and par level guide](/blog/pharmacy-reorder-point-par-levels)** walks through recalculating them.

## Working With Wholesalers

Most Australian pharmacies buy through a primary wholesaler under the **Community Service Obligation (CSO)** arrangements, which aim to ensure PBS medicines are supplied to all pharmacies within set timeframes. Deliveries are frequent — often daily — so most pharmacies don't need deep stock on fast-moving PBS lines.

That changes the stock strategy:

- **Hold less, order more often** on fast-moving PBS lines
- **Concentrate buffer stock** on lines prone to shortages (check the TGA's Medicine Shortage Reports Database)
- **Keep a close eye on returns terms** — credits for short-dated or over-ordered stock are usually limited

## Expiry Control: Australia's Silent Margin Killer

With frequent deliveries, it's easy for a new carton to be shelved in front of an older one. Over months, the older stock expires at the back of the shelf.

The fix is **First-Expiry, First-Out (FEFO)** at both the shelf and the till:

1. Record batch and expiry at receiving
2. Shelve by expiry, not by arrival
3. Have your POS select the nearest-expiry batch automatically
4. Review a 90-day expiry report monthly

## GST and the Retail Floor

Australian pharmacies sell a mix of **GST-free** items (most prescription and many therapeutic medicines) and **taxable** items (cosmetics, many vitamins, general retail). Getting this wrong at the till creates BAS headaches later.
